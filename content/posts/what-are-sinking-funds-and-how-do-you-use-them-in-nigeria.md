Some expenses are not really surprises. You know the school fees are coming. You know Christmas will require money. You know your rent renewal is three months away. The problem is not that these costs catch you off guard. The problem is that they find you unprepared.

A sinking fund is how you fix that.

It is one of the most practical savings tools available, and you do not need a special account or a large income to use one.

## What a Sinking Fund Really Is

A sinking fund is money you set aside gradually, every month, for a specific upcoming expense.

That is the whole idea. You identify something you know you will need to spend money on in the future, calculate roughly how much it will cost, divide that amount by the number of months you have before you need it, and save that portion every month until the date arrives.

When the expense comes, the money is already there. No scrambling. No borrowing. No cleaning out your general savings and starting from zero.

The name sounds technical, but the concept is simply planned saving with a purpose attached.

## Why Many People Struggle With Planned Expenses

Here is a pattern many people will recognise.

You know rent is due in four months. You plan to sort it out closer to the time. Then three months pass, and other things have absorbed the money. Now the deadline is close and the amount feels enormous because it all needs to come from one or two months of income rather than four.

Or Christmas arrives every December, as it always does, and yet many families find themselves scrambling, borrowing, or overspending on credit because the spending was not planned for during the quieter months earlier in the year.

The root issue is usually not irresponsibility. It is that most people save in a vague, general way without assigning money to specific upcoming needs. The money sits in one pool, feels available for daily life, and gets used before the big expense arrives.

Sinking funds solve this by making the future expense visible and concrete from the moment you start saving for it.

## Sinking Fund vs Emergency Fund vs General Savings

These three things serve different purposes, and confusing them is one of the most common money mistakes people make.

**An emergency fund** exists for unexpected, unplanned events. Job loss, a sudden medical bill, a burst pipe, a family crisis that demands immediate money. You do not know when these things will happen or exactly how much they will cost. The fund is there to protect you from being completely exposed.

**A sinking fund** is for expenses you already know are coming. Rent renewal, school fees, a planned trip, a phone you know needs replacing, annual subscriptions, family events. The timing and rough cost are known in advance. You are simply spreading the financial weight across many months so it does not crush you all at once.

**General savings** usually refers to money being accumulated toward a longer-term goal without a fixed deadline, or money set aside without a specific purpose yet assigned. A house deposit you are building toward over several years, for example, or money you are growing before deciding how to use it.

All three can exist at the same time. They are not competing with each other. A person can have an emergency fund, three active sinking funds, and a general savings pot running in parallel, even on a modest income.

## Step-by-Step Guide to Creating and Using Sinking Funds

### Step 1: List the planned expenses coming up in the next twelve months

Think through every significant cost you can reasonably anticipate. Do not limit yourself to the obvious ones.

Common examples for Nigerians include rent or annual rent renewal, school fees, Christmas or Eid spending, travel costs, car maintenance or repairs, phone replacement, generator fuel or maintenance, medical check-ups, annual subscriptions, family celebrations like weddings or naming ceremonies, and household items that wear out and need replacing.

Write them all down. Be honest about what you know is coming even if you have been avoiding thinking about it.

### Step 2: Estimate the cost for each expense

You do not need a perfect figure. A reasonable estimate is enough to start.

If your rent renewal is ₦600,000 and it is due in six months, that is the figure you work with. If you plan to spend roughly ₦80,000 on Christmas, write that down. If a phone replacement is likely to cost somewhere around ₦200,000, use that as your working number.

Estimates will not always be exact. That is fine. The goal is direction and consistency, not perfection.

### Step 3: Calculate how much to save each month

Divide the estimated cost by the number of months remaining before you need the money.

Rent of ₦600,000 in six months means saving ₦100,000 per month toward that fund. Christmas spending of ₦80,000 in eight months means setting aside ₦10,000 monthly. A ₦200,000 phone replacement in ten months means ₦20,000 per month.

This is where the simplicity of sinking funds becomes clear. A large, intimidating expense becomes a manageable monthly number.

### Step 4: Open a separate place to keep each fund

This step matters more than it sounds.

When sinking fund money sits in the same account as your daily spending, it quietly disappears. Keeping it separate, even just in a clearly labelled piggy bank account or a sub-wallet feature on a banking app, creates a psychological boundary that makes the money harder to spend casually.

Many Nigerian banks and fintech platforms now offer sub-account features or savings pockets that allow you to label and ring-fence specific amounts. Use these if they are available to you. If not, even a separate savings account at your bank works fine.

The label is important. Seeing "Rent Fund — ₦300,000 of ₦600,000" is more motivating and more protective than seeing a single lump sum that could be spent on anything.

### Step 5: Move money into each fund at the start of the month

The moment income arrives, move the sinking fund contributions before spending on anything discretionary. Not after. Not later.

If you wait until the end of the month to fund your sinking accounts, life will have spent the money by then. The contributions need to move early, the same way you would pay a bill.

### Step 6: Leave the money alone until the expense arrives

This is the part that requires the most discipline, especially in difficult months.

If the fund is clearly labelled and kept separate, the temptation to dip into it is lower. Remind yourself that this money is already spent, in a sense. It is spoken for. Using it for something else just means the original expense will return as a crisis.

## Common Sinking Fund Mistakes to Avoid

**Starting too late.** The later you start, the larger the monthly contribution needs to be. A six-month fund is more manageable than a two-month scramble. If an expense is twelve months away, starting now makes it effortless. Starting two months before makes it painful.

**Mixing sinking funds with general spending.** Without separation, the money gets absorbed into daily life. Keep sinking funds distinctly apart from your main account.

**Setting unrealistic targets.** If you budget ₦5,000 per month toward a fund that actually needs ₦150,000 in three months, the plan will fail. Be honest with your estimates and your timeline. If the numbers do not work, either extend the timeline, reduce the target where possible, or adjust other parts of your budget.

**Borrowing from one fund to cover another.** This creates a chain of gaps. If you pull ₦30,000 from your rent fund to cover an unrelated expense, your rent fund is now short and will need to catch up. Keep funds for their stated purpose.

**Forgetting to update the fund as costs change.** If rent increases at renewal, update the target and adjust your monthly contribution. A sinking fund is a living plan, not a set-and-forget arrangement.

## How to Start If Your Income Is Small or Irregular

If your monthly income is limited, you may not be able to fund every sinking fund at once. That is completely normal.

Start with the one or two most urgent upcoming expenses. Focus your available saving capacity there first. As other expenses come closer in time, you can start those funds or reallocate once earlier targets are met.

For irregular income earners, the approach is slightly different. Rather than committing to a fixed monthly amount, contribute a percentage of whatever comes in. When a higher-income month arrives, make larger contributions. When a quieter month comes, contribute what you can and make up the difference when income recovers.

The key principle remains the same: direct money toward a specific named goal before it disappears into general spending.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many sinking funds can I have at once?** As many as make sense for your situation. Some people run two or three. Others manage five or six. The practical limit is your ability to contribute meaningfully to each one every month. If contributions become too small to matter, reduce the number of active funds.

**Can I use a regular savings account for a sinking fund?** Yes. Any account where the money stays separate from your daily spending works. Some people use sub-savings features on banking apps. Others use a basic savings account at their bank. The label and the separation matter more than the account type.

**What if the expense ends up costing more than I saved?** Top it up from your monthly buffer if possible, or from general savings. Then adjust the target and contribution for next time. A sinking fund that covers most of an expense still helps. It is better than having nothing.

**Is a sinking fund the same as a savings goal?** They are similar. A sinking fund is a savings goal with a specific deadline and a clear monthly contribution tied to it. The term "savings goal" can be broader. The mechanics are the same.

**What happens to the money if the expense does not happen?** You keep it. If you saved toward a trip that did not happen, the money can stay in that fund for a future trip, roll into another fund, or move into general savings or your emergency fund. It is still your money.
