You are a tailor. Or a hairdresser. Or a POS agent. Or a food vendor. You work for yourself. When work is plenty, money comes in. When work is slow, money stops. And the rent still comes. The materials still cost money. The shop still needs to open.

That is the problem with working for yourself. Your income goes up and down. But your costs do not.

A cash reserve is the answer. It is money you set aside for the slow months. Not money for a new phone. Not money for a party. Money that sits and waits, so a bad month does not kill your business.

## Why you need two reserves, not one

Most advice says: build an emergency fund. Save 3 to 6 months of your expenses. That advice was written for salary earners. For you, it is not enough. You need two reserves.

The first is a **business cash reserve**. This money keeps the business alive. It pays for rent, materials, transport, and data when customers are few. Without it, one slow month can close your shop. With it, you can wait for work to come back.

The second is a **personal emergency fund**. This money keeps your house running. It pays for food, school fees, and medicine when your personal income drops. Our guide to [how to build an emergency fund with irregular income](/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund-with-irregular-income-in-nigeria/) covers that one in full.

Why business first? Because if the business dies, the personal fund will not last long. The business is what brings money in. Protect it first. Then build the personal fund.

## Step 1: Work out one month of business costs

You cannot build a reserve if you do not know the number you are aiming for. So the first step is simple: write down what your business costs in one month.

Do not guess. Open your notebook or your phone. Look at last month. Write down every business cost. Here is an example. All numbers below are **illustrative only** — your own numbers will be different.

Take Amina, a tailor in Ibadan. She sews clothes from a small shop. Her business costs in one month look like this:

| Business cost | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Shop rent (her share for the month) | ₦20,000 |
| Materials (cloth, thread, zips) | ₦50,000 |
| Transport (to the market and to customers) | ₦15,000 |
| Phone and data | ₦5,000 |
| Other small costs (needles, pressing, repairs) | ₦10,000 |
| **Total for one month** | **₦100,000** |

Check the maths: ₦20,000 + ₦50,000 is ₦70,000. Add ₦15,000 and you get ₦85,000. Add ₦5,000 and you get ₦90,000. Add ₦10,000 and you get ₦100,000. One month of business costs is ₦100,000.

Your own list will look different. A POS agent pays for float and data, not cloth. A hairdresser pays for cream, attachments, and light. A food vendor pays for gas, foodstuff, and transport. The items change. The method does not. Knowing your costs also stops a common mistake: thinking all the money that came in is profit. Our guide on [turnover is not profit](/turnover-is-not-profit-how-traders-separate-business-money/) explains that trap.

Be honest when you list your costs. If you leave out the small ones — the ₦500 here, the ₦1,000 there — your number will be too small, and your reserve will run out too fast. If you are not sure about a cost, write your best guess and correct it next month.

## Step 2: Set your reserve target

Once you know one month of business costs, you can set a target. A practical target is **1 to 3 months of business costs**. One month covers one bad month. Two months covers a slow season. Three months is strong — it covers a real problem, like a broken machine.

This is a practical target, not a rule from any bank or regulator. Pick the one you can reach. A target you can reach is better than a big target you give up on.

For Amina, one month of business costs is ₦100,000. So her targets are:

- 1 month: ₦100,000
- 2 months: ₦200,000
- 3 months: ₦300,000

She chooses 2 months. Her target is ₦200,000. That is the number she is building towards.

Do not try to save the whole target at once. That is not how a reserve is built. It is built small, many times. Which brings us to the most important step.

## Step 3: Save a fixed share of every payment, first

Here is the rule that makes it work: **every time money comes in, take a fixed share for the reserve before you spend anything.**

Not at the end of the month. Not from what is left. First. Because if you wait for what is left, there is usually nothing left.

Pick a share you can keep. Ten percent is a good start. That means for every ₦10,000 that comes in, ₦1,000 goes to the reserve.

Amina's shop brings in about ₦250,000 in a normal month. All figures here are **illustrative**. At 10%, she saves ₦25,000 a month for the reserve. Her target is ₦200,000. So:

₦200,000 ÷ ₦25,000 = 8 months.

In 8 months, her reserve is full. That sounds slow. It is slow. But slow is how reserves are built. And after 8 months, she has something most self-employed people do not have: two months of business costs sitting and waiting.

What if she can save 15% instead? That is ₦37,500 a month. Then ₦200,000 ÷ ₦37,500 is about 5 and a half months. Faster. But only pick 15% if you can truly keep it up in a slow month too. A share you keep is better than a bigger share you abandon.

What if your income is small and uneven? Then save a smaller share. Even 5% is a start. On ₦100,000 of income, 5% is ₦5,000. It will take longer. But the habit matters more than the speed. A person who saves ₦5,000 every time will reach the target. A person who waits for a big month will still be waiting next year.

This is the same idea behind [how to manage irregular income](/how-to-manage-irregular-income-in-nigeria/): pay yourself and your reserve first, then plan the rest.

## Step 4: Keep the reserve in a separate account

This step is simple, and many people skip it. Do not skip it.

Keep the reserve in a **separate account** — a different bank account, or a separate savings wallet — that you do not use for daily spending. If the reserve sits in the same account as your daily money, you will spend it. Not because you are careless. Because it is there, and the need will always feel urgent.

The account should be easy to reach when the business truly needs it, but hard to reach for everyday spending. No ATM card for it in your wallet, if you can avoid it. Check it once a month, not every day.

Tell yourself the rule in plain words: this money is for the business in a bad month. It is not for food, not for clothes, not for a new phone. It is for keeping the shop open.

If you have not yet separated your business money from your personal money, do that first. Our guide to [how to separate personal and business money](/how-to-separate-personal-and-business-money-in-nigeria/) shows how to set up that split. The reserve needs its own corner. Without the split, there is no corner.

## Step 5: Use it only for the business, then rebuild it

A reserve is not a trophy. It is meant to be used. The point is to use it at the right time, and then rebuild it.

Use the reserve when the business truly needs it:

- A slow month, and the rent is due.
- Your machine breaks, and you cannot work without it.
- You need materials for a big job, and the customer will pay after delivery.

Do not use it for personal spending. If the house needs money, that is what the personal emergency fund is for. Mixing the two is how both disappear.

And when you use it, rebuild it the same way you built it: a fixed share of every payment, until it is full again. Do not wait for a good month to start rebuilding. Start with the next payment that comes in.

Amina's story, again with **illustrative** numbers: in her ninth month, business goes slow. She uses ₦80,000 from her reserve to pay rent and buy materials. Her reserve drops from ₦200,000 to ₦120,000. She does not panic. She goes back to her rule: 10% of every payment. At ₦25,000 a month, she is back to ₦200,000 in about 3 months — ₦80,000 ÷ ₦25,000 is a little over 3 months. The reserve did its job, and then she built it back.

That is the whole system. Build it. Use it when you must. Build it again.

## What if you have a loan or a debt?

Some self-employed people owe money. Should they build a reserve, or pay the debt first?

Do both, in this order: keep a small business reserve first — even one month of costs — then put extra money towards the debt. Why? Because if the business stops, you cannot pay the debt at all. A small reserve protects the income that pays the debt.

If the debt is a loan app that charges high fees, the debt is urgent too. Our guide to [how to break a loan-app rollover cycle](/how-to-break-a-loan-app-rollover-cycle-in-nigeria/) explains how to stop borrowing to repay borrowing.

## A simple weekly check

You do not need a complicated system. Once a week, answer three questions:

1. How much came in this week?
2. Did I move my fixed share to the reserve?
3. How much is in the reserve now?

Write the answers in a notebook. That is your [weekly cash flow forecast](/weekly-cash-flow-forecast-for-small-business-in-nigeria/) in its simplest form. When the reserve grows, you will see it. When it stops growing, you will know something changed — and you can fix it early.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should my business reserve be?

A practical target is 1 to 3 months of your business costs. Work out one month of costs first, then multiply. Start with 1 month if money is tight. Aim for 2 or 3 months when you can. There is no official rule — pick a target you can reach and keep.

### I earn very little. Can I still build a reserve?

Yes. Save a smaller share — even 5% of every payment. On ₦100,000 of income, that is ₦5,000. It will take longer, but the habit is what matters. A small reserve that exists is better than a big reserve that never gets built.

### Should the reserve be in a bank account or in cash at home?

A separate bank account or savings wallet is better. Cash at home is too easy to spend, and it is not safe. A separate account keeps the money out of your daily spending, but you can still reach it when the business truly needs it.

### What if I use the reserve and cannot rebuild it?

Go back to the rule: a fixed share of every payment, even a small one. If your income has dropped for a long time, lower the share instead of stopping. Stopping is what kills the reserve. A smaller share keeps it alive.

### Is a business reserve the same as an emergency fund?

No. A business reserve keeps the business alive in a slow month. A personal emergency fund keeps your house running. You need both. Build the business reserve first, because the business is what brings the money in, then build the personal fund. See our guide to [how to build an emergency fund with irregular income](/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund-with-irregular-income-in-nigeria/) for the personal side.

## The bottom line

Working for yourself means your income goes up and down. Your costs do not. A cash reserve is what stands between those two facts.

Build it in this order: work out one month of business costs, set a target of 1 to 3 months of that number, save a fixed share of every payment before you spend anything, keep it in a separate account, and rebuild it the same way after you use it.

Start small. Start with the next payment that comes in. A slow month will come — that is certain. Whether it closes your business is not. That part is up to the reserve you built.
