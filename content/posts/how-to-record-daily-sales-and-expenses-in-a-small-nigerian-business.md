A provision-shop owner receives ₦80,000 from customers in one busy day. At closing, only ₦37,000 is left in cash. Did the business lose money? Was there a theft? Or did stock purchases and transfers account for the difference?

Without a daily record, the answer is a guess. **Tracking sales is not the same as tracking profit**, and neither is the same as seeing how much cash is available to spend.

This guide gives a simple, realistic method for Nigerian traders, caterers, artisans and other small businesses. You can use a ruled notebook, spreadsheet or existing bookkeeping app.

## The five columns to start recording

At minimum, keep these separate:

| Entry | What goes there |
| --- | --- |
| Sales made | Value of goods/services sold that day |
| Cash actually received | Cash, bank transfers and POS receipts credited |
| Direct cost of sales | Stock or materials used to generate those sales |
| Operating expenses | Transport, power, data, rent, packaging and other overhead |
| Owner withdrawals | Money personally taken out of the business |

If customers buy on credit, **record the sale and the amount still owed separately**. A promised transfer should not be counted as cash already received.

For a fuller explanation of managing owner withdrawals, see [separating personal and business money](/how-to-separate-personal-and-business-money-in-nigeria/).

## A worked example: one day's records

Imagine a shop that sells ₦80,000 of goods and receives the entire amount that day.

| Item | Hypothetical amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Sales revenue | ₦80,000 |
| Cost of stock sold | ₦50,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **₦30,000** |
| Electricity and generator fuel | ₦4,000 |
| Transport and delivery | ₦3,000 |
| Packaging and phone data | ₦3,000 |
| **Illustrative operating profit** | **₦20,000** |

That ₦20,000 is a simplified operating profit **before any other expenses, taxes, loan repayments or adjustments not shown**. It is not necessarily what you should take home.

If the shop must pay a supplier tomorrow or replace goods before opening, you still need a cash-flow plan.

## Why buying stock is tricky

If you buy ₦100,000 of stock today but sell only ₦50,000 of its cost value, the unsold stock is still an asset of the business, not automatically a complete loss for today's profit calculation.

Likewise, the **cash paid for stock** and the **cost of goods actually sold** can be different. Your cash ledger tracks what leaves the account; your stock record helps calculate profit.

If you are beginning with a notebook, do not worry about perfect accounting software. But keep these concepts distinct so you do not mistake a temporary cash shortage for an unprofitable product.

## The five-minute end-of-day routine

1. Write down all cash, transfer and POS receipts; do not double count one sale made through two messages.
2. Add outstanding customer credit balances separately.
3. Record cash spent on stock, delivery, power, staff and other business needs.
4. Count actual cash in the till and compare it with expected cash after petty-cash payments.
5. Reconcile transfers against the bank statement, not only customer screenshots.

If the totals differ, create a **cash variance** entry and investigate. Do not quietly adjust the sales number just to make the difference disappear.

## How to record transfers and POS payments

A screenshot of a payment may not mean money reached your account. Confirm through your bank or verified payment-provider records before releasing goods where required.

Record the amount paid, channel, fee actually charged, transaction reference and settlement date.

If a transfer is missing, use the appropriate [NIP trace guide](/how-to-find-nip-session-id-for-bank-transfer-in-nigeria/) or [failed-transfer dispute steps](/bank-transfer-failed-but-debited-in-nigeria/). Don't count a disputed payment as confirmed cash.

## Weekly totals tell a better story

At the end of seven days, add the numbers and compare:

- Total sales made versus total cash actually collected.
- Total stock cost for goods sold.
- Operating expenses.
- Customer credit outstanding.
- Supplier payments due.
- Owner withdrawals.

A week of good sales can still produce a cash shortage if customers pay late and suppliers demand payment early. That is why a [weekly cash-flow forecast](/weekly-cash-flow-forecast-for-small-business-in-nigeria/) complements this ledger.

## Common bookkeeping mistakes

**Mixing personal expenses with business costs.** If you take money to buy household groceries, record an owner withdrawal rather than pretending the groceries were business stock.

**Counting every deposit as revenue.** A loan, owner capital injection or customer refund is not necessarily a sale.

**Ignoring payment-platform charges.** A ₦10,000 sale with a fee credited as ₦9,950 needs both the sale and fee recorded.

**Relying on memory.** At the end of a busy market day, five small purchases can disappear from your recollection.

**Treating bank balance as profit.** The balance doesn't show unpaid rent, future stock requirements or unpaid customer invoices.

## Sample notebook format

| Date | Item or sale | Received | Paid out | Category | Reference |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| Monday | Customer payments | ₦32,000 | — | Sales receipt | Transfer/cash |
| Monday | Stock purchase | — | ₦20,000 | Inventory | Supplier slip |
| Monday | Generator fuel | — | ₦3,000 | Overhead | Receipt |
| Monday | Owner withdrawal | — | ₦2,000 | Personal draw | Journal |

These figures show cash movement, not a complete profit calculation. Keep a separate simple stock sheet if you sell physical products.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I keep business records in a notebook?

Yes, if you update and reconcile them reliably. Protect the notebook and back up important figures.

### Should I record credit sales as cash?

No. Track the sale and accounts receivable separately; cash arrives only when the customer actually pays.

### Is money paid to myself a business expense?

An owner's draw may not be an operating expense for accounting or tax purposes. Record it separately and seek professional guidance on the treatment appropriate for your business structure.

### What if I don't know the cost of goods sold?

Start tracking purchase prices, quantities bought and quantities sold. An approximate figure is not a replacement for proper inventory records, but a transparent estimate can reveal what data you still need.

## The bottom line

A daily record should answer **what was sold, what was received, what was spent, what remains owed and what the owner took out**. Once you can see those numbers, you can plan tomorrow's stock and stop confusing turnover with income.
