You sell goods all month. At the end of the month, you count it all: ₦500,000. You feel rich. Then you go to buy new stock, and the money is not there. Rent is due. School fees are due. Where did ₦500,000 go?

It did not disappear. Most of it was never yours. That ₦500,000 is your turnover. It is not your profit.

## Three words you must not mix up

Learn three words once, and your business will make sense.

**Turnover.** All the money customers paid you. If you sold goods worth ₦500,000 in a month, your turnover is ₦500,000. It says nothing about what you kept.

**Cost of goods.** This is what you paid for the goods you sold. If you bought rice, noodles, and drinks for ₦340,000, and you sold all of them, your cost of goods is ₦340,000.

**Profit.** What is left after you remove the cost of goods and all your shop bills. Profit is the only number that tells you whether the business is working.

Turnover minus cost of goods minus expenses equals profit. That is it.

## A real month, worked out step by step

Take Mama Ngozi, a provision shop owner who sells rice, oil, noodles, and drinks. In one month, her numbers look like this. Every number below is **illustrative only** — your numbers will differ, but the method is the same.

| What happened | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Money from customers (turnover) | ₦500,000 |
| Cost of the goods she sold | −₦340,000 |
| **Money left after cost of goods (gross profit)** | **₦160,000** |
| Transport to market | −₦15,000 |
| Shop rent for the month | −₦20,000 |
| Light and power | −₦8,000 |
| Bags and packaging | −₦5,000 |
| POS and bank charges | −₦4,000 |
| Phone and data | −₦3,000 |
| **Total shop bills (expenses)** | **−₦55,000** |
| **Profit left (net profit)** | **₦105,000** |

Check the maths: ₦500,000 − ₦340,000 = ₦160,000. The shop bills add up to ₦55,000. And ₦160,000 − ₦55,000 = ₦105,000.

So Mama Ngozi sold ₦500,000 of goods, and her profit is ₦105,000. That is the truth of the month. Good money — but not ₦500,000.

If she spends like she made ₦500,000, she will eat her stock money, and next month she will not buy enough goods. That is how most small shops die: not from one bad month, but from spending turnover as if it were profit.

## The daily ledger: five minutes that tell you the truth

You do not need an app or a computer. A notebook works. Every evening, write four numbers for the day.

Here is a small example from Mama Ngozi's notebook. Every number is **illustrative only**.

| Date | Item sold | Money in | Cost of goods | Money out | Profit left |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Rice, oil, noodles | ₦18,000 | ₦12,000 | ₦2,000 | ₦4,000 |
| Tue | Drinks, provisions | ₦22,000 | ₦14,500 | ₦2,500 | ₦5,000 |
| Wed | Rice, drinks | ₦15,000 | ₦10,000 | ₦1,500 | ₦3,500 |
| **Total for 3 days** | | **₦55,000** | **₦36,500** | **₦6,000** | **₦12,500** |

Check it: Monday ₦18,000 − ₦12,000 − ₦2,000 = ₦4,000. Tuesday ₦22,000 − ₦14,500 − ₦2,500 = ₦5,000. Wednesday ₦15,000 − ₦10,000 − ₦1,500 = ₦3,500. Totals: ₦55,000 − ₦36,500 − ₦6,000 = ₦12,500.

Copy this table into your notebook and use it every day. **Money in** is all the money customers paid you that day. **Cost of goods** is what you paid for the goods you sold — if you bought a carton for ₦10,000 and sold half, write ₦5,000. **Money out** is shop bills paid that day. **Profit left** is money in, minus cost of goods, minus money out.

At the end of the month, add up the profit-left column. That is your profit for the month.

Write it the same day. Do not trust your memory — by Sunday, you will not remember Tuesday's sales. ## Pay yourself a fixed salary

Here is the part most traders skip. You work in the shop every day, so you deserve pay. But pay yourself from the profit, not from the sales.

Go back to Mama Ngozi. Her profit is ₦105,000. She pays herself a fixed salary of ₦60,000, on the same day every month, like a worker receives salary. She moves it from the business account to her personal account, and that is her house money.

What is left in the business? ₦105,000 minus ₦60,000 is ₦45,000. That money stays in the business. It buys new stock and covers slow weeks.

Compare that with dipping into the till: ₦5,000 today for food, ₦10,000 tomorrow for a family need, a little more the next day, and the next. By month end, you have taken ₦90,000 without knowing it. The business has nothing left to grow, and you still feel broke.

A fixed salary fixes that. You know what you will get. The business knows what it will keep. When the month is hard, adjust by decision — maybe the salary drops to ₦50,000 that month.

How much salary? It must be less than your profit. If your profit is ₦105,000, a salary of ₦60,000 leaves ₦45,000 for the business. Never take more than the profit, or you are eating the stock money.

Also, keep the money apart. A second bank account for the business is best. If you cannot open one yet, use a separate purse or wallet for business money only. Business money and house money must not sit in one place. A food vendor, a POS agent, and an online seller can use the same system. The POS agent's turnover is all the money that passed through the machine — her profit is only the charges she earned, minus her costs. The online seller's profit is what is left after the cost of the goods, delivery, and data. Same maths. Different shop.

## When house money and shop money mix

Three things cause most of the trouble.

**Food from the shop.** You sell foodstuffs, and your family eats from the shop. A bag of rice here, oil there. It feels free, but the shop paid for those goods. Every bag you eat is stock you cannot sell.

The fix: write it down. At the end of the week, add up what the family took at the shop's selling price. If they ate ₦10,000 of goods, write ₦10,000 as money you took from the business. It is part of your salary, not extra.

**Rent and school fees.** Rent is due, and the only money at hand is in the shop. School fees are due, and the business account has money. So you take it. One time becomes every time, and the business never builds a reserve.

The fix: plan for it. School fees come every term. Rent comes every year. Put a small amount aside from your salary every month for fees and rent, so the business is never the emergency fund. If you must take money for an emergency, write it down and pay it back from your next salary.

**"I will replace it when I sell more."** This is the most dangerous sentence in a trader's life. You take stock money today and promise to replace it when sales come. But sales money is turnover, not profit — when it comes, it must buy new stock first. The replacement never happens.

The fix: replace it the same week, from your personal money, or count it as part of your salary for that month. If your salary is ₦60,000 and you took ₦15,000 extra, your salary that month was ₦75,000 — and the business kept only ₦30,000.

## What to do this week

Start now, with five small steps. If your income goes up and down, our guide on [how to manage irregular income in Nigeria](/how-to-manage-irregular-income-in-nigeria/) shows how to smooth it out, and a [weekly cash flow forecast](/weekly-cash-flow-forecast-for-small-business-in-nigeria/) helps you see tight weeks before they arrive.

1. **Open your notebook.** Rule the four columns: money in, cost of goods, money out, profit left.
2. **Work out last month.** Add up your sales, your cost of goods, and your bills. Find your real profit.
3. **Set your salary.** Pick a fixed amount, less than your profit, and a fixed day to pay it.
4. **Separate the money.** Business money in one account or purse. House money in another.
5. **Count what the house takes.** Food from the shop, fees, rent — write it all down for one month. You will see the leak.

## Frequently asked questions

### My turnover is ₦500,000. How much profit should I expect?

There is no fixed number — it depends on what you sell and what your costs are. Work out your own numbers with the method in this guide. Do not copy another trader's profit.

### I sell on credit. Customers owe me. Is that money part of my turnover?

No — not until they pay. Turnover counts money customers actually paid you. If a customer took goods worth ₦20,000 and has not paid, that money is not in your hand. Write it down as money owed to you, and follow up.

### What if my profit is too small to pay myself a salary?

Pay yourself a small salary, and look at the numbers. Can you raise prices, buy stock cheaper, or cut a bill? A small salary beats no system. If the business makes almost no profit month after month, something must change — your prices, your costs, or what you sell.

### Should I use a separate bank account, or is a notebook enough?

A notebook is enough to start. A separate bank account is the next step, and it makes the separation real. If you can open one, do it. If not, use a separate purse or wallet and keep the rule just as strict.

### My business is small. Is all this maths really necessary?

Yes, and the smallest businesses need it most. A big company can survive a bad month. A small trader cannot. Five minutes a day with a notebook is the cheapest insurance your business will ever have.

## The bottom line

Turnover is not profit. Sales money is not your money until you remove the cost of the goods and pay the shop bills. Do the maths every month, write it down every day, pay yourself a fixed salary from the profit, and keep business money apart from house money. Then you will know — not guess — whether your business is working.
