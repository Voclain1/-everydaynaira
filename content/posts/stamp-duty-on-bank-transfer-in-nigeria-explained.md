You get a credit alert. Someone sent you money — maybe a friend paying you back, a client settling an invoice, or a family member sending something across. A few seconds later, another alert comes in. A debit. Small amount. The description reads something like "stamp duty" or "electronic receipt levy."

You did not ask for it. You did not approve it. And nobody told you it was coming.

If your first thought was *"did my bank just take money from me for no reason?"* — you are not alone. Stamp duty deductions on bank accounts confuse a lot of Nigerians. Some people assume the bank invented it. Others think it is a system error. A few spend time on hold with customer care, only to get a half-explanation that leaves them more confused than before.

This article breaks it down in plain language. What stamp duty on bank transfers in Nigeria actually is, why it gets deducted, when it applies, and exactly what to do if you think something went wrong.

## What Stamp Duty on Bank Transfer in Nigeria Means

Stamp duty is a tax. It has existed in Nigeria — and in many countries — for a long time. The original idea was simple: when you signed an important document like a contract or receipt, the government charged a small fee and stamped the document to show the tax had been paid.

That physical stamp is largely gone now. But the tax has not disappeared — it has moved into the digital banking system.

The Stamp Duties Act governs how stamp duty works in Nigeria. Through the Finance Acts of 2019 and 2020, the government extended the scope of stamp duty to cover certain electronic transactions. In practice, this means that when qualifying transfers happen through the banking system, a small levy is automatically collected — no paper, no stamp, just a quiet deduction on your account statement.

When you see "stamp duty," "electronic receipt levy," or "ERD" on a statement, this is what it refers to. It is a statutory charge that your bank is legally required to collect and remit to the government. It is a tax — not a service charge, not a processing fee.

## Is Stamp Duty a Bank Charge or a Government Levy?

It is a government levy. This distinction matters more than it might seem.

When your bank charges you for SMS alerts, card maintenance, or account management, that money goes to the bank. It is paying for something the bank provides. Those charges are worth understanding too — if you want a clear breakdown, see our [banking basics guide](/banking/) for an overview of how Nigerian bank accounts and their costs work.

Stamp duty is different. Your bank identifies the qualifying transaction, deducts the amount automatically, and remits it to FIRS. The bank's role is purely that of a collection agent. The money flows directly to the government.

This is why, if you call your bank to complain about stamp duty, they can explain it but generally cannot waive it the way they might reverse an error on a service fee. A correctly applied government levy follows official dispute channels — not a simple customer care request.

Understanding this one thing clears up most of the confusion: stamp duty is a government levy collected through your bank, not a bank fee.

## When Stamp Duty May Apply in Nigeria

The most widely known application has been a **₦50 charge on electronic receipts of ₦10,000 and above**.

So if someone transfers ₦10,000 or more into your account, a ₦50 deduction may appear on your statement shortly after the credit lands.

A few things to understand clearly:

**It typically applies to the receiving side.** You can send ₦20,000 to someone and see no stamp duty on your own statement. The person receiving that money is the one who sees the ₦50 deduction. The levy is on the receipt, not the sending. This surprises most people.

**Not all transaction types are treated the same.** Personal transfers, business transactions, and certain account types may be handled differently depending on the applicable rules at the time. The picture is more layered than a single blanket rule.

**The rules have changed over time.** The Finance Act 2019, Finance Act 2020, and subsequent directives have each adjusted the framework. Always check the current official guidance from FIRS or your bank's published tariff if you are trying to verify a specific charge — not information written several years ago.

**Thresholds and rates are set by government, not banks.** Whatever amount applies is determined by policy, not by what an individual bank decided.

## Does Every Bank Transfer Attract Stamp Duty?

No. Not every transfer triggers a stamp duty deduction.

The levy applies when a transaction meets the qualifying conditions — primarily the received amount crossing the applicable threshold. Transactions below that threshold have generally not attracted the charge.

Think of it this way: stamp duty is not a flat tax on every naira that moves through the banking system. It is a levy that activates when specific conditions are met. If you make several small transfers throughout the month, you may never see stamp duty at all. If you receive one transfer of ₦10,000 or above, you may see a single ₦50 deduction tied to that credit.

The deduction is per qualifying transaction, not a recurring monthly fee.

## Why Stamp Duty Appears on Your Account Statement

Your bank's system is configured to identify transactions that meet the qualifying criteria. When a qualifying receipt hits your account, the system automatically calculates and deducts the levy — no manual decision, no discretion. It is built into how the bank processes compliant transactions.

The label on your statement can vary from bank to bank:

- Stamp duty
- Electronic receipt levy
- Govt levy / stamp duty
- ERD charge
- Electronic receipt duty

Different labels, same charge. If you see a small deduction — usually ₦50 — shortly after receiving a transfer of ₦10,000 or above, that is almost certainly what it is.

## What to Do If You Think Stamp Duty Was Deducted Wrongly

Errors do happen. Stamp duty can be applied incorrectly — for example, if the transaction amount was below the qualifying threshold, if the same transaction was charged twice, or if a system error misclassified the transaction type.

Here is a clear path if you think something went wrong:

### Step 1: Pull the details before you call

Know the exact date, the credit amount, and the stamp duty amount deducted. Confirm whether the incoming transfer was ₦10,000 or above. Having these specifics ready makes the conversation faster and more useful.

### Step 2: Contact your bank first

Your bank can look at the transaction directly and explain why the deduction was triggered. Ask specifically which credit triggered it, whether the amount met the qualifying threshold, and how it was calculated. Most of the time, this conversation gives you a clear answer.

### Step 3: Ask for written confirmation if needed

If the explanation is unclear, ask the bank to confirm it in writing. This creates a record and signals you are taking the matter seriously.

### Step 4: Use formal complaint channels if unresolved

Every licensed bank in Nigeria is required to have a formal complaint process. If customer care cannot resolve it, escalate through that process and document each step you take.

### Step 5: Escalate to the CBN Consumer Protection Department

If the bank does not resolve it satisfactorily, the Central Bank of Nigeria's Consumer Protection Department handles complaints that have gone unresolved through the bank's own process. You can reach them through the CBN's official website.

If you have ever faced a debit you did not recognise or approve, our guide on [what to do after an unauthorized debit](/what-to-do-after-an-unauthorized-debit-in-nigeria/) walks through the full dispute process step by step.

### Step 6: Contact FIRS for policy questions

If your question is about whether a type of transaction should attract stamp duty at all, rather than about a specific error, that is a policy question FIRS is best placed to answer directly.

One important point: if stamp duty was correctly applied, it generally cannot be reversed. The bank has already remitted it to the government. But a genuine error — wrong amount, wrong transaction, duplicate charge — is a legitimate dispute worth pursuing properly.

## FAQ

**Why was stamp duty deducted from my account when I received money?** Stamp duty on electronic receipts applies on the receiving side. When someone transfers ₦10,000 or more into your account, a levy is automatically deducted by your bank on behalf of the government. The money goes to FIRS, not to your bank.

**Why did I see stamp duty but the person who sent the money did not?** The charge applies to the receipt, not the sending. The person receiving the qualifying amount sees the deduction, not the person who sent it. This is the opposite of what most people expect, which is why it creates so much confusion.

**Is ₦50 always the stamp duty amount?** A ₦50 charge on receipts of ₦10,000 and above has been the widely applied rate. However, rates and thresholds are set by government policy and can change. Always confirm the current applicable rate with your bank or FIRS rather than assuming a fixed amount applies indefinitely.

**Can I get the stamp duty charge reversed?** If it was correctly applied, generally no — the bank has already remitted it to the government. But if there was a genuine error, such as a charge on a transaction below the threshold or a duplicate deduction, you can raise a formal complaint and a confirmed error should be reversed.

**Who should I contact first — my bank or FIRS?** Start with your bank. They can identify exactly which transaction triggered the charge and whether it was correctly applied. For broader policy questions about whether your type of transaction should qualify at all, FIRS is the right authority.

**Does stamp duty apply to transfers between accounts in the same bank?** This depends on how current policy defines qualifying transactions and how your bank has implemented the rules. Contact your bank directly to clarify how they handle same-bank transfers in relation to stamp duty.

**What if the label on my statement is different from what this article describes?** Banks use slightly different descriptions — "ERD," "electronic receipt levy," "govt levy," and other variations all refer to the same type of deduction. If you are unsure, ask your bank to confirm what the specific entry on your statement means.

**Does stamp duty apply every time I receive money?** No. It applies when the transaction meets the qualifying conditions, primarily the amount crossing the applicable threshold. Smaller transfers that do not meet the threshold generally do not trigger the charge.

## Conclusion

Stamp duty on bank transfers in Nigeria is one of those charges that confuses people not because it is complicated, but because nobody explains it. You see a small deduction after a credit, with a label that means nothing to you, and no warning that it was coming.

Now you know what it is. A government levy — not a bank fee — collected on qualifying electronic receipts and remitted to FIRS. Your bank is acting as a collection agent. The money does not stay with them.

If the deduction makes sense given what you received, there is nothing further to do. If something looks wrong — the amount does not add up, the charge appeared twice, or the transaction seems like it should not qualify — follow the steps above and raise it formally.
