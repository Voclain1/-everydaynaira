Your phone buzzes — a WhatsApp message from a number you do not know.

"Good morning. Congratulations! You have been selected for a remote customer service job. Salary ₦350,000 per month. Work from your phone. To secure your slot, pay a ₦15,000 training fee today. Only three slots left."

Or the other version: "Invest ₦50,000 and get ₦100,000 in five days. Guaranteed. Send me a DM now before the offer closes."

It sounds exciting — and a little too easy. That feeling is worth listening to.

WhatsApp is where many Nigerians now find work and hear about money — and scammers know it. A borrowed profile picture and a promise is enough.

This guide shows you how to spot the trap before you pay, what to do in the first five minutes if you have already replied, and how to check an offer is real.

## The seven red flags

You do not need all seven to walk away — one strong red flag is enough.

### 1. They are rushing you

"Pay in the next 30 minutes or the slot goes to someone else." "The investment window closes tonight."

Real employers do not hire on a countdown, and real investment firms do not take money on one. The rush is deliberate — it stops you from thinking or checking with anyone else. A genuine offer will still be there tomorrow morning.

### 2. You have to pay before you earn

A training fee. A registration fee. A "refundable" deposit to activate your account.

Here is the simple rule: **a real employer pays you, not the other way round.** If a job asks you to pay before you have worked a single day, treat it as a scam until proven otherwise. The same goes for an investment charging an "activation fee" before you can withdraw profit — that fee is usually the whole point of the scam.

### 3. The money goes to a personal account

The "HR manager" gives you a personal account in an individual's name. The "investment company" asks you to transfer to a personal account, not a company account.

A real company pays into, and pays out from, an account in the company's name. A personal account is not proof of fraud on its own, but with urgency and a fee attached, it is a strong warning sign. Check the name — does it match the company in the message? If not, stop.

### 4. The returns are too good and too fast

"Double your money in five days." "₦20,000 becomes ₦200,000 in two weeks." "Guaranteed returns, no risk."

No honest investment doubles your money in days with no risk — returns and risk move together. And if this really worked, why would a stranger on WhatsApp need your ₦50,000? A scheme that needs new people constantly paying in collapses when the new money stops.

### 5. The profile is cloned or brand new

Look closely: the profile picture is a company logo, the number is not the one on the company's website, the account was created recently, and the name is close to a real company — but not quite.

Scammers copy the names and photos of real companies. A familiar logo proves nothing — check the number against the contact details on the company's own website, not the number in the message.

Banks never ask for your PIN or one-time code in a chat — see our guide on [fake bank customer-service numbers](/how-to-identify-fake-bank-customer-service-numbers-in-nigeria/).

### 6. They push you off WhatsApp onto a strange link

"Click this link to complete your registration." "Download this app to start earning."

The link leads to a page that looks real, or asks you to download an app from outside the official app store — it can steal the password you type in, or install something harmful.

Do not click links from strangers. If a company is real, find its website yourself. Never download an app from a link in a chat — use your phone's official app store.

### 7. They ask for your BVN, PIN, password, or one-time code

No employer, bank, or investment company will ask for your BVN, card PIN, bank password, or one-time code — not on WhatsApp, not on a call, not by email. Anyone who asks wants into your account.

## What to do in the first five minutes if you already replied

Maybe you have already chatted with the person, or even sent money. Do not panic, and do not let shame slow you down — the next five minutes matter most.

**Minute 1: Stop.** Do not send more money, and do not pay a "withdrawal fee" or "tax" to release your profit — that is the second half of the same scam. Do not send your BVN, PIN, or any code, whatever they threaten.

**Minute 2: Screenshot everything.** Save the chat, the number, the profile picture, the promises, the account details, and your receipt. Export the chat if you can — evidence is much harder to recover after the number is blocked or the messages are deleted.

**Minute 3: Block and report the number.** In WhatsApp, open the chat, tap the contact, and use Block and Report.

**Minute 4: Tell your bank.** Call your bank's official support line or open its verified app. Give them the amount, the receiving bank and account, the date, and the transaction reference. Ask them to open a suspected-fraud complaint, explain what recall or investigation steps are available, and give you a complaint or tracking number.

**Minute 5: Change anything you exposed.** If you shared a password or code, or typed bank details into a strange website, change your passwords from a trusted device and tell your bank.

Our guide on [what to do after sending money to an online scammer](/what-to-do-after-sending-money-to-an-online-scammer-in-nigeria/) covers the evidence bundle and what to expect next.

## Authorised by deception is not the same as unauthorised

If you **authorised a payment because you were deceived** — you approved the transfer yourself — report it as suspected fraud. Recovery is not guaranteed, and nobody can honestly promise you a reversal. Your bank may liaise with the receiving bank, but it depends on what is still in the account.

If money left your account **without your consent**, that is an unauthorised debit — a different report. See [what to do after an unauthorised debit](/what-to-do-after-an-unauthorized-debit-in-nigeria/).

Do not describe an authorised payment as a network failure or bank error — accurate facts make the investigation easier. If your complaint is about how your bank handled your case, the [CBN consumer complaints process](/how-to-report-a-bank-to-cbn-in-nigeria/) may also be relevant, but it is not a substitute for reporting the suspected crime itself.

## How to verify a real job or investment

Before you pay anything, run these five checks — a real offer passes them; a scam usually fails at the first.

**1. Find the company yourself.** Search for the company name yourself. Does it have a real website, a real address, and contact details matching the WhatsApp number? If the only trace of the company is the message itself, walk away.

**2. Check the job advert's source.** Real jobs are usually advertised on the company's own careers page or a known job board. A job that exists only in a WhatsApp broadcast deserves suspicion.

**3. Ask for a written offer.** A real employer sends a written offer letter — company name, role, salary, start date — before asking you to pay for anything. If the "HR manager" refuses to put anything in writing, that is your answer.

**4. Check whether an investment operator is registered.** In Nigeria, investment schemes and the firms offering them are regulated, and a genuine operator can point you to its registration. Ask for the operator's full legal name and registration details, then check them through the regulator's own website — not a screenshot the person sends you. No verifiable details, no money.

**5. Ask someone you trust.** Show the message to a friend or family member before you pay. Scams rely on privacy and speed, and fresh eyes often spot in seconds what you missed in minutes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is every job advertised on WhatsApp a scam?

No. Some genuine small businesses and recruiters do use WhatsApp. The question is not where you saw the advert, but what it asks you to do: a real employer will not ask you to pay a fee before you start, will not rush you, and will give you verifiable company details. If those checks pass, the channel matters less.

### What if the person sent me a photo of their ID or a company certificate?

A photo of an ID or certificate proves very little — both are easy to copy, and scammers routinely send stolen or forged documents. Check the company and the offer through independent sources, rather than trusting a document sent by the person trying to take your money.

### Can my bank reverse the money if I was scammed?

Maybe, but nobody can guarantee it. Your bank can open a suspected-fraud complaint and liaise with the receiving bank, but whether money comes back depends on what is still in the receiving account and what the investigation finds. Anyone promising a guaranteed reversal — especially for a fee — is almost certainly running a second scam on top of the first.

### Should I report the scam even if I lost only a small amount?

Yes. Report it to your bank and to the relevant authorities — small losses are how many scams stay invisible. Keep your complaint number and your evidence, and do not pay any private agent who offers to file the report or freeze an account for you.

### What if the scammer is threatening me?

Threats are part of the pressure. Do not engage, do not pay to stop them, and do not share more personal information. Block the number, save the evidence, and report the matter to your bank and the appropriate authorities. If you feel unsafe, involve someone you trust and consider reporting to the police.

## The bottom line

A job that asks you to pay before you earn is not a job. An investment that doubles your money in days with no risk is not an investment. No real company needs your PIN or one-time code.

Slow down. Check the offer through a source you found yourself, not a link the stranger sent. Ask someone you trust. And if you already replied: stop, screenshot, block and report, tell your bank.

The message in your inbox is only a message. Your money leaves your account only when you say so.
