You open your banking app and check your savings balance. The interest for the month is there, a small line at the bottom of the statement. Then you see a headline: the Central Bank has decided on interest rates again. And you wonder what that actually means for your money.

That is the right question. The 23% in the headline is not the rate your bank pays you, and it is not the rate it charges you either. Here is what the decision means for your savings and your loans, and the few things worth checking at your own bank.

## What actually happened

On Tuesday, 24 November 2026, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) announced that it would hold its benchmark interest rate, the Monetary Policy Rate (MPR), at 23%. The decision came at the end of the Monetary Policy Committee's 308th meeting, held on 23 and 24 November in Abuja. The vote was unanimous.

This was the last scheduled meeting of the year, and the outcome was widely expected. The MPR had only just been cut in September 2026, from 26.5% to 23%. This time, the committee chose to wait and see how that cut works its way through the economy. It also left everything else unchanged: the standing facilities corridor at +50 and -300 basis points around the MPR, and the Cash Reserve Requirement at 45% for deposit money banks, 16% for merchant banks, and 75% for public sector deposits outside the Treasury Single Account.

In plain terms: nothing moved.

## What the MPR is, in plain English

The MPR is the rate around which the Central Bank lends to banks and takes deposits from them. Think of it as the wholesale price of money in the banking system.

When a bank needs cash overnight, it can borrow from the Central Bank at just above the MPR — 23.5% at the current rate, because the corridor adds 50 basis points on top. When a bank has spare cash overnight, it can deposit it with the Central Bank at 300 basis points below the MPR, or 20%. So the MPR sets a band, and what banks pay savers and charge borrowers is priced around it, with each bank adding its own margin.

The Cash Reserve Requirement is the other lever. At 45% for deposit money banks, banks must keep 45 out of every 100 naira of qualifying deposits with the Central Bank, rather than lending it out — one reason lending stays expensive even when the benchmark rate falls.

Remember: the MPR is a direction, not a price tag. It does not fix the rate on your savings account or your loan — your bank decides those, within rules set by the Central Bank.

## What it means for your savings

Here is the one place where the MPR connects directly to your money. Under a Central Bank rule in force since August 2022, banks must pay at least 30% of the MPR on ordinary savings accounts. That minimum is called the savings floor.

At an MPR of 23%, the floor is **23% × 30% = 6.9% a year**. Because the committee held the rate, the floor stays at 6.9%. It does not go up, and it does not go down. A cut would have lowered it; a rise would have raised it.

What does 6.9% actually look like? The table below is illustrative — it assumes the money sits untouched for a full year, the bank pays exactly the floor, and no fees or tax are taken off.

| Savings balance | Interest in one year at 6.9% |
| --- | --- |
| ₦100,000 | ₦6,900 |
| ₦250,000 | ₦17,250 |
| ₦500,000 | ₦34,500 |
| ₦1,000,000 | ₦69,000 |

First, your bank may pay more than the floor. The 6.9% is a minimum, not a fixed rate, and the only way to know what you get is to check your own account. Savings apps and Treasury bills set their rates differently again, so compare options before moving money — our guide to [where to keep your savings in Nigeria](/where-to-keep-your-savings-in-nigeria/) walks through the trade-offs.

Second, interest is paid before deductions. [Bank charges](/bank-charges-in-nigeria-explained/) and small fees such as [SMS alert charges](/sms-alert-charges-in-nigeria/) come out of the same account your interest goes into, and on a small balance they can eat a noticeable share of it.

Third, inflation. Headline inflation was 14.06% in October 2026, down from 14.63% in September and 15.39% in August, according to the National Bureau of Statistics. Money earning 6.9% is growing in naira but shrinking in what it can buy — roughly 7 percentage points behind inflation over the year. That is not a reason to stop saving, but do not treat a savings account as an investment. Its job is safety and access.

## What it means for your loan

If you were hoping the CBN decision would make loans cheaper, here is the disappointing part: a hold changes nothing, and even a cut would probably not help as quickly as you might expect. Your loan rate is not the MPR. Banks take the benchmark as a starting point, then add a spread to cover their operating costs, the risk that a borrower may not repay, and their profit. The spread is large: Nairametrics has reported lending rates of roughly 20% to 46% a year across banks, measured while the MPR was at 23%.

The September cut is the clearest proof: when the MPR fell from 26.5% to 23%, lending rates did not fall with it, at least not immediately. Banks reprice loans slowly and price each borrower individually.

So what should you take from the hold? Three things.

First, if you already have a loan, your rate almost certainly stays where it is. A fixed-rate loan does not change with the MPR at all, and a hold gives your bank no reason to review a variable one.

Second, if you are about to borrow, do not shop by the headline rate. Shop by the total cost. Two loans with the same advertised rate can cost very different amounts once fees, the repayment method, and the loan term are counted. [How loan interest works in Nigeria](/how-loan-interest-works-in-nigeria/) explains the pieces, and the difference between [flat rate and reducing balance](/flat-rate-vs-reducing-balance-loan-interest-in-nigeria/) alone can change what you repay by tens of thousands of naira.

Third, be careful with the cheapest-looking offer. A loan that is easy to get is often the most expensive one once the full repayment is added up. If the lender cannot show you the total amount you will repay, in writing, before you accept, that is your answer.

## What to do now

A rate hold is not a moment for dramatic moves. It is a moment for a short check-up.

**1. Check what your bank actually pays you.** Find the interest rate on your savings account or last statement — not the balance. If it is exactly 6.9%, your bank is paying the floor. If it is higher, good. If you cannot find the rate at all, ask.

**2. Work out what your interest is worth after charges.** Add up a month of account charges and SMS alert fees and compare it with a month of interest. If charges are eating most of the interest, turn off paid alerts you do not need, or move the money to the account where it earns the most and costs the least.

**3. If you are borrowing, get the full repayment figure.** Before you accept any loan, ask for the total you will repay, including fees, and the monthly repayment for the full term. Compare that total across lenders, not the advertised percentage.

**4. Do not move your emergency fund chasing a slightly higher rate.** The difference between 6.9% and, say, 8% on ₦200,000 is ₦2,200 over a whole year — not worth losing instant access to money you might need.

**5. Watch the next decision, not this one.** The committee will meet again, and the next move could be a cut. If that happens, the savings floor will fall with it — that is the moment to re-check your rate.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the MPR?

The MPR, or Monetary Policy Rate, is the Central Bank of Nigeria's benchmark interest rate — the rate around which the Central Bank lends to banks and takes deposits from them. It is set by the Monetary Policy Committee, which meets several times a year.

### Will my savings interest go up because the CBN held the rate at 23%?

No. A hold means nothing changes: the minimum interest on an ordinary savings account stays at 6.9% a year. Your rate only rises if your bank chooses to pay above the floor, or if the CBN raises the MPR at a future meeting.

### Does the 23% rate mean my bank must pay me 23% on my savings?

No. The 23% is the benchmark rate, not a savings rate. The rule for ordinary savings accounts is that banks must pay at least 30% of the MPR — 6.9% a year. Banks may pay more, but none is required to pay 23%.

### Will my loan become cheaper now?

Almost certainly not. The committee held the rate, so there is no change to pass on. Even when the MPR was cut in September 2026, from 26.5% to 23%, lending rates did not fall immediately. Loan rates depend on each bank's costs, the borrower's risk, fees, and the repayment method — not just the benchmark.

### Why is the interest on my savings so much lower than the interest on a loan?

Because the two rates are set differently. Your savings floor is 30% of the MPR — 6.9% at the current rate. A loan rate is the MPR plus a spread for the bank's costs, the risk of non-repayment, and profit. That spread is why lending rates of roughly 20% to 46% a year have been reported across banks while the savings floor sits at 6.9%.

## The bottom line

The Central Bank held its rate at 23%, unanimously, at its last scheduled meeting of 2026. For your savings, the floor stays at 6.9% a year — ₦34,500 on ₦500,000 left untouched for a year, before deductions, and still behind inflation. For your loan, nothing changes, and even a future cut would take time to reach your repayments.

The headline is not the decision that matters most. The numbers that matter are on your own statement: the rate your bank actually pays you, the charges it actually takes, and the total you would actually repay on any loan. Check those, and the CBN's next decision will be something you can respond to, rather than something you have to guess about.
