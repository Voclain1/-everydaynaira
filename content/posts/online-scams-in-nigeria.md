## Common Scams in Nigeria in 2026: What to Watch Out For and How to Stay Safe

You get a message on WhatsApp. It looks like it is from your bank. The logo is correct, the language sounds official, and the message says your account will be restricted in 24 hours unless you click a link to confirm your details.

Or maybe you are scrolling Instagram and you see a vendor selling quality shoes at a price that looks like a genuine sale. You pay. Nothing arrives.

Or someone you went to school with messages you on Facebook after years of silence, quickly turns the conversation to an investment opportunity, and sends you a link that promises 50 percent returns in two weeks.

In 2026, scams in Nigeria are not rare events that happen to other people. They arrive in your inbox, your SMS, your social media feed, your WhatsApp, and sometimes at the POS machine right in front of you. They are faster, more targeted, and harder to spot than they used to be.

This guide breaks down the most common scams Nigerians are dealing with right now, explains how each one works, what the warning signs look like, and what you should do to protect yourself.

## Why Scams Are Still a Major Problem in Nigeria in 2026

More Nigerians now use mobile banking, make payments online, and engage on social media daily. That growth has also created more opportunities for fraud. Scammers follow the money, and in 2026, the money is digital.

Two things have made scams more convincing than ever. First, the technology to fake a bank logo, clone a website, or spoof a phone number is now cheap and easy to access. Second, many scams now work by creating urgency, so your instinct to act quickly works against you.

The Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC) continues to issue public warnings about evolving fraud patterns targeting ordinary Nigerians. Commercial banks fraud team and the Nigerian Communications Commission (NCC) have also raised consistent alerts about phishing, SIM swap, and account takeover attacks.

Understanding what scams actually look like in practice is the most effective protection you have.

## The Most Common Scams in Nigeria in 2026

### 1. Fake Bank Alerts and Phishing Messages

This is one of the oldest scams in Nigeria, but it is still one of the most effective.

You receive an SMS or email that looks exactly like a message from your bank. It may say your account has been flagged, your BVN needs verification, your account is about to be suspended, or a suspicious transaction has been detected. The message includes a link. That link takes you to a fake website designed to look like your bank's portal. Once you enter your details, the scammer has everything they need to empty your account.

**Why people fall for it:** The message looks genuine. The logo, the font, the language, and even the sender name can look correct. When a message creates panic, the natural response is to act fast.

**Warning signs:**

- The link does not end in the official bank domain (for example, gtbank.com, accessbankplc.com, uba.com)
- The message asks you to click a link to verify your details
- There is urgency, a countdown, or a threat of account suspension
- The message comes from a random phone number, not an official bank short code
- There are spelling errors or slightly wrong grammar

**What to do:**

- Do not click any link in the message
- Open a browser separately and go directly to your bank's official website by typing the address yourself
- Call your bank's official customer care number, which is usually printed on the back of your debit card
- If in doubt, visit a branch

### 2. WhatsApp Impersonation Scams

Someone messages you pretending to be a friend, a family member, your boss, or a colleague. They may have taken over that person's actual WhatsApp account, or they may be messaging you from a new number claiming they lost their phone. Within a short time, they are asking for money urgently. Sometimes they claim to be stuck somewhere, in a hospital, or unable to access their own account.

A related pattern involves fake customer care accounts. You comment on a bank or fintech's official social media post asking for help, and within minutes a fake customer care account messages you privately, asking for your account details or OTP to "resolve your issue."

**Why people fall for it:** The name and possibly the profile photo match someone you know. The story creates sympathy and urgency. Fake customer care accounts look almost identical to real ones.

**Warning signs:**

- A known contact messages you from a new number asking for money as a first or very early message
- They ask you not to call, saying the call function on their new phone is not working
- A "customer care" account contacts you first, rather than you reaching out to them
- The account has very few followers, was created recently, or has a slightly different spelling in the handle

**What to do:**

- Call the actual person through their known number or another verified contact
- Never send money without voice verification
- Banks and fintechs never ask for your OTP, PIN, or full card number through DM
- Block and report the fake account

### 3. Fake Online Stores and Shopping Scams

A vendor on Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, or WhatsApp shows you products at attractive prices, often clothes, phones, electronics, or food. You pay. Then silence, or a run of excuses, or partial delivery of something completely different.

In some cases, the scammer operates a fake website that looks like a real store. The site accepts payment but the goods never ship. In other cases, they are active on social media with real-looking posts and even testimonials, but the reviews are staged.

**Why people fall for it:** Social media shops look legitimate. A good-looking Instagram page with lots of posts feels like proof of a real business. Prices that seem like discounts can override caution.

**Warning signs:**

- No verifiable address, phone number, or business registration visible
- They insist on bank transfer only, no escrow or protected payment option
- Prices are significantly below market rate
- Testimonials all look similar or were posted in a short window
- They pressure you to pay quickly before the offer "expires"
- The account is new or has very few interactions relative to its follower count

**What to do:**

- Search the vendor's name and number on Google before paying
- Ask for a video call to show the product live before purchase
- Look for their CAC number or verifiable business address
- Use a payment method with a dispute option where available
- Check if other buyers have tagged them in reviews on separate posts

### 4. Fake Investment, Ponzi, Crypto, and Forex Scams

Someone shows you a platform, a group, or an "opportunity" that promises returns well above what any legitimate investment offers. In 2026, these scams regularly use crypto, forex trading, binary options, and AI-trading as cover language. They often work through WhatsApp groups, Telegram, and social media pages. Members are encouraged to bring in others, and early participants receive payouts from new members' funds — the classic Ponzi structure.

Some of these platforms look professional. They may have polished apps, trading dashboards, and even fake certificates. They often start by paying out small amounts to build trust before the big crash.

**Why people fall for it:** Early investors often genuinely receive money, so they become honest believers who convince others. The desire for financial progress is real, and when someone you trust vouches for a platform, caution drops.

**Warning signs:**

- Returns promised are unusually high, often 20 to 100 percent in weeks
- Urgency to join before a slot closes
- Pressure to recruit others
- No clear explanation of how profits are generated
- Not registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission Nigeria (SEC Nigeria)
- Withdrawal problems appear after several months
- The platform is only accessible through a link, not a verifiable app store listing

**What to do:**

- Always check if an investment platform is registered with SEC Nigeria at sec.gov.ng
- Ask clearly how the platform makes money. If there is no clear answer, walk away
- Be skeptical of any platform promoted primarily through WhatsApp groups with testimonials
- Do not invest money you cannot afford to lose into any unverified platform

### 5. Job and Recruitment Scams

A job offer arrives that looks almost too convenient. It may come through LinkedIn, email, WhatsApp, or a fake job portal. The offer is usually for a well-paying role that requires little experience. Once you express interest, you are asked to pay a registration fee, training fee, or processing fee. After payment, either nothing happens or they string you along asking for more money.

A variation of this targets people seeking remote work. The scammer hires you, then sends a "mistaken" payment far larger than your agreed amount and asks you to refund the difference. The original payment later bounces, but your refund has already left your account.

**Why people fall for it:** Job scarcity makes any offer feel worth exploring. Fake job listings often use real company names and logos.

**Warning signs:**

- Unsolicited job offer with no application process
- Request for payment before employment starts
- Vague job description but very specific payment promise
- Interview conducted entirely over WhatsApp or Telegram
- The salary is strikingly high for the apparent requirements
- The company cannot be verified through official search

**What to do:**

- Never pay any money to get a job
- Verify the company exists by checking their official website and contacting them directly through publicly listed details, not through the number that messaged you
- Check for the company on the Corporate Affairs Commission portal at cac.gov.ng
- Legitimate companies do not conduct final recruitment over WhatsApp

### 6. OTP and Card Fraud

You receive a call from someone claiming to be a bank employee, a telecom agent, or an EFCC official. They already know some of your details, perhaps your name, partial account number, or even your BVN. They say they need your OTP to reverse a suspicious transaction, upgrade your account, or prevent a debit.

Once you share the OTP, they use it to authorize a transfer out of your account. In seconds, your money is gone.

POS-related fraud also continues to be a problem. This can involve card skimming devices at ATMs, swapping your card for a fake while you are distracted, or tricking you into entering your PIN multiple times under the cover of a "failed transaction."

**Why people fall for it:** The caller sounds official. They already know your name and some details, which feels like proof they are who they say. The urgency of stopping a suspicious transaction pressures quick action.

**Warning signs:**

- Any call or message asking for your OTP, no matter who they claim to be
- A POS transaction that "fails" multiple times
- A stranger who lingers too close while you are using an ATM
- Your ATM card returned to you and then you are distracted before leaving

**What to do:**

- Never share your OTP with anyone. Your bank will never ask for it
- Cover your PIN at all ATMs and POS terminals, every time
- Check your card after every POS transaction — confirm it is yours before leaving
- If a transaction fails more than once, call your bank before trying again
- Activate transaction alerts on your account so you see movement in real time

### 7. Fake Giveaways and Promo Scams

A post circulates that a popular brand, a telecom company, or a celebrity is giving away money, phones, or vouchers. All you need to do is click a link, share the post, or provide your account details to claim your prize. Sometimes you are told you have won a competition you never entered.

**Warning signs:**

- The post asks you to share before you can "unlock" the prize
- The link does not match the official brand domain
- You are asked for your bank details, BVN, or PIN to receive your prize
- The post has unusually high shares but very few comments from real people
- The brand's official verified account has not made any mention of the promo

**What to do:**

- Verify any promo on the official verified account of the brand
- Never enter banking details to claim a prize
- Be suspicious of any offer that requires you to share it first — that is designed to spread the scam, not reward you

### 8. Delivery and Dispatch Scams

You ordered something online, or you receive a message saying a package is waiting for you. The message asks you to click a tracking link or pay a customs clearance fee before delivery can proceed. The link is fake. The fee disappears. There is no package.

A related version involves someone sending a payment notification to a seller that looks like a real bank alert but is a screenshot or a fake SMS. The seller releases the goods before the money actually arrives.

**Warning signs:**

- You receive a delivery notification for something you did not order
- A customs or clearance fee is required before delivery, especially through a personal account
- A buyer's alert message asks you to check a link rather than your banking app

**What to do:**

- Always confirm credit in your banking app directly, not through a screenshot or SMS
- Do not release goods until you have seen the money in your balance
- For delivery notifications, go directly to the courier's official website to track using the number provided — do not click links in messages

## Red Flags That Apply to Almost Every Scam

Across all of these scam types, certain patterns repeat. Train yourself to slow down when you notice any of these:

- Someone is creating urgency or a deadline
- The offer is unusually good for what is required
- You are being asked to keep something secret
- You are being asked to pay before receiving anything
- The request involves your OTP, PIN, BVN, or card number
- You cannot verify the identity of who you are dealing with through an independent channel
- Your gut is saying something is off

That last one matters more than people admit. Scams work partly because people second-guess their instincts. If something feels wrong, slow down before you act.

## How to Protect Yourself From Scams in Nigeria

1. **Use two-factor authentication** on email, banking apps, and social media accounts where available.
2. **Set transaction limits** on your bank accounts so that even if someone gains access, they cannot move large amounts in one go. Most Nigerian banks allow you to set daily limits through the app or by calling customer care.
3. **Keep your BVN private.** Your BVN is linked to all your bank accounts. Be careful who you share it with and in what context.
4. **Use separate accounts** where possible. Keep a small-balance account for online shopping and social media transactions, and keep your main savings in an account with stricter access.
5. **Verify before you pay.** Whether it is a seller, a job offer, or a charity, take five minutes to verify independently before sending money.
6. **Talk to someone before acting on big decisions.** Scammers rely on isolation and speed. A quick conversation with a trusted person can break the spell.

## What to Do If You Have Already Been Scammed

Being scammed is not a sign of weakness or stupidity. These schemes are designed by people who study human psychology, and they are often convincing. What matters most now is moving quickly.

**Step 1: Call your bank immediately.** If the fraud involved your account, call your bank's fraud hotline right away. Most banks can freeze transactions or escalate a reversal request if you report quickly enough. The number is usually on the back of your debit card.

**Step 2: Block or freeze your card.** Use your banking app to block your card if you suspect your card details have been compromised.

**Step 3: Change your passwords and PINs.** If you shared any login details, change them across all related accounts immediately.

**Step 4: Document everything.** Save the messages, transaction references, phone numbers, screenshots, and any other evidence. You will need these when reporting.

**Step 5: Report the fraud.** See the section below.

**Step 6: Alert your contacts.** If a scammer used your identity or account, let people in your network know so they are not targeted next.

## Where to Report Scams in Nigeria

1. **Your bank's fraud team** Your first call should always be to your bank. Nigerian banks are required to have fraud response channels. The number is on your card or the bank's official website.
2. **Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC)** The EFCC handles financial fraud. You can report online at efcc.gov.ng or call their hotline. They also accept reports through their social media pages.
3. **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)** For complaints about banking services and unresolved fraud issues with your bank, you can contact the CBN Consumer Protection Department at [cpd@cbn.gov.ng](mailto:cpd@cbn.gov.ng). Complain to your bank first and include its complaint reference number.
4. **Nigerian Communications Commission (NCC)** For telecom-related scams involving SIM swaps, number spoofing, or SMS phishing, contact the NCC at ncc.gov.ng or their consumer line.
5. **Securities and Exchange Commission Nigeria (SEC Nigeria)** For investment fraud and Ponzi scheme complaints, file a report at sec.gov.ng.
6. **Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC)** For fake or abusive loan apps, debt shaming, and misleading consumer offers, report at fccpc.gov.ng. Since 2025, every digital lender must be registered with the FCCPC.
7. **Nigeria Police Force** For offline fraud or if you can identify the scammer, you can report to the nearest police station or through the NPF's cybercrime reporting channels.

Keep a record of every report you make, including dates and reference numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a scammer steal my money just from knowing my bank account number?** Your account number alone is not enough to move money out of your account. What scammers need is your OTP, card PIN, internet banking password, or similar authentication detail. Protect those, and your account number alone cannot be used to steal from you. That said, do not share your account details more broadly than necessary.

**Is it safe to pay sellers through bank transfer on Instagram?** Bank transfer to unknown sellers carries real risk. If the seller turns out to be fraudulent, the money is very difficult to recover. Try to use payment methods that offer some buyer protection where possible, or verify the seller thoroughly before paying.

**How do I check if an investment platform is registered in Nigeria?** Go to sec.gov.ng and use their investor protection tools to search for registered operators. If a platform is not on that list, be very cautious about investing any money with them.

**What should I do if I accidentally shared my OTP?** Call your bank immediately. Tell them what happened and ask them to freeze your account or block further transactions while they investigate. The faster you call, the better your chances of limiting the damage.

**Can WhatsApp scammers use my identity to scam my contacts?** Yes. If a scammer takes over your WhatsApp account or clones your number, they can and do message your contacts impersonating you. If your account is taken over, alert your contacts through another channel immediately and contact WhatsApp support to recover your account.

**Are all online stores on Instagram and Facebook unsafe?** Not all of them. Many legitimate small businesses sell successfully through social media. The key is to verify before paying. Look for a CAC registration, a verifiable address, reviews on separate platforms, and a willingness to do a video call showing the product. When in doubt, ask for references.

**How do I know if a bank alert SMS is real?** Real bank alert SMS messages typically come from a short code matching your bank, such as a five or six-digit code. They do not contain clickable links asking you to log in. They show a transaction reference and your updated balance. If a message asks you to click a link to verify, confirm, or undo a transaction, do not click it. Go to your banking app directly.

**I reported a scam but nothing seems to be happening. What should I do?** Fraud investigations can take time. Keep your documentation ready and follow up. You can escalate bank complaints to the CBN if the bank is not responding satisfactorily. For EFCC complaints, you can follow up through their official channels. Persistence matters.

## Conclusion

Scams in Nigeria in 2026 are more sophisticated than they were even three years ago, but the core of every scam is still the same: someone is trying to rush you into an action before you can think clearly.

Your best defence against scam is not suspicion of everyone. It is the habit of slowing down when something feels unusual, verifying independently before you act, and knowing exactly what you should never share — your OTP, your PIN, your password, and your card details.

If you have been scammed, do not wait and hope. Contact your bank, document what happened, and report it. You may not get every naira back, but your report helps others.
