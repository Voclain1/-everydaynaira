A small business has paid customers in its sales records but owes ₦90,000 to a supplier on Friday. Several clients promise to pay next week. The business may be profitable on paper and still not have enough cash for that Friday bill.

A **weekly cash-flow forecast** helps you see the timing problem. It is not the same as a profit statement or a guess about how much you will earn.

This guide is for Nigerian small shops, service businesses, freelancers and growing side hustles that need to plan the next four weeks.

## The basic formula

**Closing cash = Opening cash + Money expected to arrive − Cash payments due.**

The closing amount becomes the following week's opening cash.

Use money that is genuinely available to the business. Do not mix a supplier's advance, a personal emergency fund or a loan approval that has not been disbursed into available cash.

## A four-week cash-flow example

Consider a small service business with ₦40,000 cash at the start of Week 1:

| Week | Opening cash | Collections expected | Payments due | Closing cash |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | ₦40,000 | ₦80,000 | ₦90,000 | ₦30,000 |
| 2 | ₦30,000 | ₦65,000 | ₦70,000 | ₦25,000 |
| 3 | ₦25,000 | ₦100,000 | ₦75,000 | ₦50,000 |
| 4 | ₦50,000 | ₦70,000 | ₦85,000 | ₦35,000 |

These are **illustrative amounts**, not industry averages. The week-by-week arithmetic is based on the formula above.

The business never reaches negative cash in this scenario. But a ₦30,000 delay in Week 2 collections would turn its expected ₦25,000 closing balance into **minus ₦5,000**, revealing a shortfall that needs a plan.

## What belongs in cash coming in?

Include customer payments you expect to **actually receive during the week**, not every invoice you issue.

Cash inflows can include paid sales, expected transfer settlements, confirmed client milestone payments and other identifiable cash receipts.

Separate amounts you are reasonably confident about from uncertain promises. For example:

| Client payment | Amount | Expected timing | Confidence |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Completed job with written payment date | ₦50,000 | Week 2 | More predictable |
| Verbal promise from overdue client | ₦25,000 | Week 2 | Uncertain |
| Sale not yet made | ₦20,000 | Unknown | Do not count as confirmed |

A forecast can include scenarios, but you should label assumptions. For an operational cash decision, build a conservative version without optimistic collections.

## What belongs in cash going out?

Include dated bills such as restocking, shop rent, salaries, supplier deposits, fuel, data, delivery costs, loan repayments and known taxes or fees.

Avoid treating the *cost of goods sold* as cash leaving on the same day if you actually paid for the stock earlier. The forecast follows **payment dates**, while the profit calculation follows different accounting concepts.

## Build the forecast in six steps

1. Confirm opening cash from statements and counted cash, less money you cannot use.
2. List bills due in each of the next four weeks.
3. Add expected customer payments by their likely **receipt dates**, not invoice dates.
4. Calculate each week's closing balance.
5. Flag weeks that drop below the cash needed for business continuity.
6. Reconcile actual payments weekly and roll forward the plan.

A notebook can work. If using a spreadsheet, make Week 2's opening cash a reference to Week 1's calculated closing cash. This reduces arithmetic mistakes.

## What to do when a week goes negative

A shortfall does not automatically mean the business has failed. It is a warning about timing or inadequate cash.

Check whether some customer payments can be collected earlier under agreed terms, whether non-essential purchases can wait, or whether suppliers offer a lawful written payment arrangement.

If you consider borrowing, compare the full cost and whether repayment will cause another negative week. The [loan calculator](/tools/#loan-calculator) can help illustrate repayments, but it does not decide whether a loan is appropriate for your business.

Never silently use money kept for employees or suppliers to fund unrelated household purchases.

## Cash flow versus profit

Suppose you sold ₦100,000 of services and incurred ₦60,000 of related costs. That suggests an illustrative ₦40,000 margin before other costs—but if the customer hasn't paid, the bank account might still be empty.

Alternatively, a customer may pay a deposit today for work you must complete next month. Cash is present, but some of it may be needed for future delivery costs or obligations.

That is why you need both a reliable [business-money separation system](/how-to-separate-personal-and-business-money-in-nigeria/) and an honest weekly cash-flow projection.

## When you run a business and earn a salary

Keep household salary and business income in distinct records. If you inject personal money into the business, label it as an owner's contribution rather than a sale.

If you withdraw money from business cash, record it and then check whether supplier and payroll commitments remain covered. See [how to pay yourself from a side hustle](/how-to-pay-yourself-from-a-side-hustle/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should the forecast cover?

Four weeks is a useful start for weekly operating decisions. Extend it for known major obligations such as annual rent, inventory cycles or seasonal demand.

### What if customers pay late?

Maintain a conservative scenario that moves uncertain collections to a later week. Do not pretend a promised payment is cash already in the bank.

### Can I use Excel or Google Sheets?

Yes. A simple formula for each week can calculate opening balance plus collections minus payments. Protect the file and reconcile it with actual statements.

### Is a positive closing balance enough?

Not always. The business may need a minimum operating reserve to cover next week's essential spending. Treat a dangerously low positive balance as an early warning.

## The bottom line

A good cash-flow forecast answers **whether money will be available when each bill falls due**, not merely whether sales look impressive. Update it weekly, especially when customer payments are uncertain.
