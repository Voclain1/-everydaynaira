A lender offers you ₦300,000 at **3% interest per month**. Another lender also says 3%. Does that mean both loans cost the same?

Not necessarily. One lender may calculate interest on the *original* ₦300,000 for all six months. Another may calculate it on the outstanding balance after every repayment. A single percentage cannot tell you which agreement is cheaper.

Let's work through the two methods without confusing the numbers with quotes from real lenders. This is an **illustrative repayment comparison**, not an advertised Nigerian loan offer.

## What is flat-rate interest?

With a simple flat-rate arrangement, interest is charged on the **starting principal for the stated period**, even while you repay parts of it.

For a hypothetical ₦300,000 loan, 3% monthly flat interest and a six-month term:

**Total interest = ₦300,000 × 0.03 × 6 = ₦54,000.**

If the lender divides principal and interest into six equal instalments:

- Principal borrowed: ₦300,000
- Flat interest: ₦54,000
- Total repayable before fees: **₦354,000**
- Monthly instalment: **₦59,000**

The monthly instalment is not an estimate: ₦354,000 divided by six equals ₦59,000. It assumes no extra processing fees and six equal instalments.

## What is reducing-balance interest?

With a reducing-balance loan, each monthly interest charge is calculated on the **principal still outstanding**.

At the same nominal 3% monthly rate, a six-month loan with **equal monthly amortised instalments** has a different result. Using the standard repayment formula:

**Payment = Principal × monthly rate ÷ (1 − (1 + monthly rate) to the power of −months).**

On ₦300,000 for six months, the payment is about **₦55,379.25 a month**. Total repayments are about **₦332,275.50**, of which approximately **₦32,275.50** is interest before fees.

Why does the interest fall? After each instalment, the principal is lower. The lender has less outstanding principal on which to charge the next month's interest.

| Six-month ₦300,000 example | Flat rate | Reducing balance |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Advertised monthly rate | 3% | 3% |
| How interest is calculated | Original principal | Principal still owed |
| Monthly repayment | ₦59,000 | About ₦55,379 |
| Interest over six months | ₦54,000 | About ₦32,276 |
| Total before fees | ₦354,000 | About ₦332,276 |
| **Difference in total cost** | **₦21,724 more** | **₦21,724 less** |

The difference is approximately **₦21,724** in this example. It is not a universal percentage saving: the result changes with the term, rate, timing and method.

The key insight is that the interest portion goes down while the principal portion of a fixed instalment goes up.

## Are flat rates always bad?

Not every flat-rate offer is automatically more expensive than every reducing-balance offer. The nominal rate could be lower, the term shorter, fees different or a lender may offer an early-repayment rebate.

The fair comparison is the **total cost of borrowing for the same amount actually received and the same repayment dates**.

For example, comparing a 2% flat monthly offer with a 5% reducing monthly offer is not the same comparison as the 3%-versus-3% table above. You must calculate both full schedules.

## Why processing fees change the result

A lender might approve ₦300,000 but deduct ₦9,000 in fees before paying you. If you receive ₦291,000 while still owing repayments calculated on ₦300,000, your effective borrowing cost is higher than the headline rate suggests.

Ask for:

- **Gross loan principal:** what the agreement says you owe.
- **Net disbursement:** what actually arrives in your account.
- **Repayment schedule:** every instalment and its due date.
- **Total interest:** all interest over the entire term.
- **Other charges:** processing, management, insurance, penalty and early-repayment fees.
- **Effective or annualised cost:** the lender's APR or equivalent disclosure, using the relevant definition.

A promised “low rate” means little without these numbers. We explain related warning signs in [our guide to dangerous loan offers](/signs-of-dangerous-loan-offers-every-nigerian-should-know/).

## What do Nigerian regulators require?

The [CBN's published Guide to Charges](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/Out/2019/CCD/Guide%20to%20Charges%20by%20Banks%20Other%20Financial%20and%20Non-Financial%20Institutions%20eff%20Jan%201%202020.pdf) includes disclosure requirements for regulated financial institutions and loan offer letters. Different types of lenders may also be subject to additional requirements. The [FCCPC's digital-lending rules](https://fccpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/DIGITAL-CONSUMER-LENDING-2025-LATEST.pdf) address digital lenders and consumer protections.

Do not assume a rule applying to a commercial bank automatically applies identically to every app or lender. Ask the lender to provide the complete written offer and identify its regulator.

If the lender refuses to show the total amount repayable or is evasive about the calculation method, pause before accepting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 3% per month the same as 36% APR?

No. Twelve times a monthly rate is a **simple annualised nominal figure**, not necessarily the true APR or effective annual borrowing cost. Repayment pattern and fees matter.

### What if I repay the loan early?

The answer depends on your contract. Reducing-balance interest may stop accruing on repaid principal, but some flat or fixed-fee loans do not automatically rebate interest. Ask for an early-settlement quote.

### Do all loan apps use flat rates?

No. Product design varies. Read the offer letter and request a repayment schedule showing principal, interest and fees.

### Is the lower monthly payment always better?

Not automatically. A longer term can make monthly repayments smaller while increasing the total interest paid. Compare the **full cost and term**.

## The bottom line

Do not choose a loan based on the interest percentage alone. Ask **what balance the rate applies to, for how long, and how much you will repay in total**.

For the illustrative ₦300,000 six-month loan, equal 3% monthly rates still produce very different totals. Getting the schedule in writing is the simplest way to see that difference before you commit.
