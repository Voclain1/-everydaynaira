Mama Aisha sells tomatoes at Mile 12 market. Every week she puts some money aside. Part of it is for next month's rent. Part of it is for her children's school fees in January. The rest is for restocking her stall.

A customer once asked her why the money just sits in her savings account. She laughed. "Because I will need it soon. I cannot play with rent money."

She is right. Money you will need in a few weeks or months is called short-term savings. It needs a safe home, and it needs to be there when you reach for it.

This guide explains the three main places Nigerians keep short-term savings: an ordinary savings account, a money-market fund, and Treasury bills. We will explain each one in plain words.

## What the CBN hold means for your savings

On Tuesday, 24 November 2026, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) held its main interest rate, the Monetary Policy Rate, at 23%. That was the decision of its 308th meeting. The rate was cut from 26.5% to 23% in September 2026. This time, the committee left it where it is.

Why does that matter to you? Our guide to [what the CBN holding rates at 23% means for your loan and savings](/cbn-interest-rate-23-percent-what-it-means-for-loans-and-savings-nigeria/) explains the loan side too. For savings, one simple rule matters. Since August 2022, banks must pay at least 30% of the main rate on ordinary savings accounts. That minimum is called the savings floor. At a main rate of 23%, the floor is 23% × 30% = 6.9% a year. Because the CBN held the rate, the floor stays at 6.9%.

Here is what that looks like in real money. All the figures in this guide are **illustrative only** — they show the maths, not a promise from any bank. If you keep ₦500,000 in a savings account for a full year, and your bank pays exactly the floor, you earn ₦34,500. That is ₦500,000 × 6.9%, before fees and tax. Your bank may pay more. Many do. But no bank is forced to pay more than 6.9% on an ordinary savings account.

But prices are still rising faster than savings interest. The National Bureau of Statistics put headline inflation at 14.06% in October 2026. So money in a savings account grows in naira, but buys less than before.

## Option 1: An ordinary savings account

This is the account most of us already have. Your salary enters it. Your POS payments leave it. You can withdraw from it at any time.

It fits money you may need tomorrow. Chidi is a salary earner in Ikeja. His rent is due in three weeks, and school fees are due in five weeks. That money should sit in a savings account. If the money has a job in the next few weeks, the savings account is the right home for it.

The strength of this option is speed. You get your money immediately. No waiting. No forms. No penalty for withdrawing.

But charges eat into your interest. SMS alert fees, transfer fees and account maintenance fees come out of the same account. Our guide to [bank charges in Nigeria](/bank-charges-in-nigeria-explained/) shows which charges are normal. And the rate is low: the floor is 6.9% a year, far below inflation. A savings account keeps your money safe and close. It does not grow it.


## Option 2: A money-market fund

A money-market fund is a pool of money managed by a professional fund manager. Many people put money in. The manager uses it to buy safe, short-term things, like Treasury bills and bank deposits. The profit is shared among everyone in the fund. You open an account with the fund manager, often through an app, and you put money in. The manager does the rest.

It fits money you will not need this week, but will need in one to six months. A teacher in Enugu is saving for school fees due in January. The money can sit in a fund for two months and earn more than a savings account. A trader in Aba sets aside money for a big restock in December.

Most funds pay you within one to three working days after you ask. That is fine if you plan ahead, but not if you need the money today. So never put emergency money in a fund. If you do not have an emergency fund yet, start with our guide to [building an emergency fund in Nigeria](/emergency-fund-nigeria/).

What did funds pay in November 2026? Fund yields change every week, and they are not guaranteed. But in the Securities and Exchange Commission's weekly table for the week ended 23 October 2026, the Stanbic IBTC Money Market Fund yielded 17.14%, and the ARM Money Market Fund yielded 16.53%. Compare that with the savings floor of 6.9%. On an illustrative ₦500,000 kept for a year at 17%, you would earn about ₦85,000, before fees and tax. At the 6.9% floor, the same money earns ₦34,500.

But remember: 17% is not a promise. It is a report of one week. If rates fall, the fund's yield falls too, and the fund takes a small fee. Always ask for the current yield and the fee before you join. If someone promises you a fixed 30% or 40% every month, walk away — that is a trap.

## Option 3: Treasury bills

A Treasury bill is a short loan you give to the Federal Government. The government borrows money for a short time — 91 days, 182 days or 364 days — and pays you interest for it. Because the borrower is the government, it is seen as one of the safest places to keep money in Nigeria. You buy Treasury bills through your bank or a stockbroker, usually when the CBN holds an auction every two weeks.

You pay for the bill upfront, at a discount. When it matures, the government pays you back the full amount. The difference is your interest. Take an illustrative 91-day bill: you pay about ₦96,000 today, and after 91 days you receive ₦100,000. The exact figures depend on the rate at the auction.

It fits money with a fixed date, at least three months away. A parent saving for school fees next term, four months away, can use a 91-day bill.

But the lock is also the risk. You only get your money back when the bill matures. You cannot withdraw it like a savings account. So never put money you may need next week into a Treasury bill.

At the Treasury bill auction of 11 November 2026, the stop rates were 15.40% for 91 days, 15.79% for 182 days, and 15.89% for 364 days. Rates change at every auction. At 15.89% for a year, an illustrative ₦500,000 would earn about ₦79,450 before fees and tax, against ₦34,500 at the savings floor. The difference is real, but the money is locked for a year.

Ask your bank what the minimum amount is, and how much tax is taken from the interest. Buy only through your bank or a licensed broker.

## A simple comparison

| Option | What you earn (Nov 2026) | How fast you get your money | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Savings account | At least 6.9% a year (the floor) | Immediately | Money you need this week |
| Money-market fund | About 16.5% to 17.1% a year (one week in Oct 2026) | 1 to 3 working days | Money you need in 1 to 6 months |
| Treasury bill | 15.4% to 15.9% a year (auction, 11 Nov 2026) | Only at maturity (91, 182 or 364 days) | Money with a fixed date, 3 months or more away |

No option is best for everything. The right choice depends on when you need the money.

## How to split your money

Ngozi earns ₦250,000 a month as a nurse in Port Harcourt. She has saved ₦300,000. Her rent of ₦150,000 is due in three weeks. School fees of ₦100,000 are due in four months. The remaining ₦50,000 is her emergency cushion. All figures below are illustrative.

- **₦150,000 for rent — savings account.** She needs it in three weeks. It must be there, in full, on the day.
- **₦100,000 for school fees — money-market fund.** Four months is long enough for the fund to earn more than a savings account, and she can ask for the money three days before the fees are due.
- **₦50,000 emergency cushion — savings account.** If her child falls sick tonight, she needs that money tonight.

She matched each pot of money to the date she needs it. That is the whole secret of short-term savings.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is the safest place to keep money I need next month?

A savings account in a licensed bank. Your money is there when you need it, and the bank must pay at least 6.9% a year.

### Is a money-market fund safe?

A fund from a registered manager is generally seen as low risk. But it is not a bank account. The return changes every week, it is not guaranteed, and you cannot withdraw at an ATM.

### Can I lose money in a Treasury bill?

Treasury bills are seen as one of the safest options in Nigeria because the Federal Government is the borrower. The main risk is locking your money away. If you need it early, you may have to sell, which takes time and may reduce your return.

### How much money do I need to start?

It depends on the provider. Fund managers and banks each set their own minimum. Ask before you decide, and compare two or three providers. Do not borrow money to meet a minimum. If the minimum is too high for you, a savings account is the right place to start.

### My savings account pays less than 6.9%. What should I do?

First, check your statement. Charges may be eating into the interest. If your bank is truly paying below the floor, ask the bank in writing. If the answer does not make sense, complain through the bank, and then to the CBN.

### Should I move all my savings out of the bank?

No. Keep the money you may need at short notice in the bank. Move only the part with a clear date three months or more away.

## The bottom line

Short-term money has one job: be there when you need it. A savings account does that best, and it pays at least 6.9% a year while the CBN holds the rate at 23%. A money-market fund pays more — around 16.5% to 17.1% in the week we checked — but you wait one to three working days for your money. A Treasury bill pays around 15.4% to 15.9% at the November auction, but your money is locked until it matures.

None of them beats inflation by a wide margin. So do not chase the highest number. Ask one question instead: when do I need this money? If the answer is this week, keep it in the bank. If the answer is in three months, a fund or a bill can earn you more. Split the money by the date, not by the rate.
